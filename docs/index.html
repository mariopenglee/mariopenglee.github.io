Mario Peng Lee

cofounder of condense.chat. making powerful AI accessible to everyone, and safe when it gets there.

I'm cofounder and chief scientist at condense.chat, which compresses what coding agents send to the model so heavy AI workloads cost less (up to about 70% off long sessions, with basically the same output). I'm also CEO of Pocketful, which brings WhatsApp AI agents to restaurant owners in Argentina. Both are basically the same bet, that AI should be cheap and usable enough for everyone.

My background is in linguistics, computer science and psychology (UCLA, Phi Beta Kappa). Mostly I'm interested in how minds (human and machine) represent things, and my view is that understanding that is how AI gets cheaper and safer. These days that means open-source interpretability research at EleutherAI through SOAR 2026 (its Summer of Open AI Research, about 2% of applicants accepted), lately on how stable sparse autoencoder features are across seeds.

Before condense I was a senior AI engineer at nexos.ai, where I built the agent layer, the memory model behind nexos.heartbeat, and a sandboxed code interpreter.

writing

  • Sparse autoencoder seeds share more than matching shows (2026-10-31): Two sparse autoencoders that differ only in seed match about half their latents one-to-one. A transplant test calibrated on toy worlds estimates the real shared share at 75 to 86%.
  • Same seed, different SAE: GPU noise and seed dependence (2026-10-31): Rerun a sparse autoencoder with the same seed and data, changing only GPU kernel nondeterminism, and it leaves 43 to 85% as many latents unmatched as a new seed, mostly the same ones.
  • Where role collapse happens in diffusion models (2026-10-23): Prompt SDXL for a mouse chasing a cat and you usually get a cat chasing a mouse. My first suspect was CLIP reading prompts as a bag of words, but after ruling out the encoder, guidance, and cross-attention, the problem seems to sit in the UNet itself.
  • Kids vs LLMs at telling a story (2026-10-18): Give a five year old and a language model the same wordless picture book. In 2024 the kids kept track of the characters far better than GPT-4. Two years of models later, the best ones tell the story just like the kids do.
  • cyclediffusion: can a picture be a bottleneck? (2026-10-17): I trained a diffusion model so a captioner could read its pictures back into the original sentence. Instead of inventing a secret visual code, it collapsed into texture, and the reason is the reader you train against.
  • A transformer that passes the wug test (2026-10-16): I fine-tuned T5 to turn spelling into IPA. Nobody told it English phonology rules, but it says "wugs" with a /z/ and "wuks" with an /s/, just like a kid does.
  • Why linguists keep writing about John and Mary (2026-10-15): In linguistics papers, 30% of male names are John and 31% of female names are Mary. A good list of names doesn't fix that, because people still pick from it. A random generator does.
  • Do sparse autoencoders capture manifolds? (2026-07-15): A concept like "year" lives on a curved manifold, and a single sparse autoencoder often shatters it instead of capturing it whole. Training several SAEs that differ only in random seed and pooling them tiles the manifold back together, right where one SAE dilutes it.
  • block-GRPO: doing RL on a diffusion language model (2026-05-09): GRPO needs a log-probability that diffusion LLMs can't give you cheaply. Using the model's own block-autoregressive structure makes most of it exact, and it beat vanilla GRPO by about 10x on tail reward.
  • Infinite context agents, at WIP London (2026-04-18): At WIP, an Index Ventures x Anthropic event for London AI builders, I presented memgit, git-backed memory that lets agents keep context across sessions instead of starting from zero.
  • Building nexos.heartbeat: the infinite context problem (2026-03-01): An always-on AI twin doesn't have a context window problem, it has an infinite context problem. Information theory, the design space, and why our memory is a git repository.
  • Code interpreter: how we built it ourselves (2026-01-30): How the nexos.ai code interpreter lets a model run real code instead of guessing: what it is for, and the isolated, ephemeral micro-VM sandbox behind it.
  • RAG isn't dead, plain RAG is (2025-12-04): Why plain RAG breaks on real enterprise knowledge, and what organizing that knowledge first (late chunking, topic clusters, models that navigate a knowledge tree) changes. With Povilas Nagrockas.
  • Context engineering: where AI stops guessing (2025-11-19): The hard part of enterprise AI isn't a bigger model, it's getting the right documents, rules and situational awareness in front of the model before it answers.
  • Psychology-inspired agentic AI (2025-11-17): Why agents inherit human failure modes, and how psychology and cognitive science (working memory, theory of mind, team size, bias) shape agent design. My write-up of the Tesonet talk.
  • Psychology for agent design, at Tesonet's AI conference (2025-11-14): At Tesonet's AI-Focused Conference in Vilnius I argued that a lot of agent failures are old psychology problems, and that organizing agents like a small team with clear roles helps.
  • GPT-2, IOI, and the sphex problem (2025-05-21): A wasp can look like it understands what it's doing right up until you move one thing. I poked at GPT-2's indirect-object circuit the same way, to see whether it understood the task or just had a routine that usually works.
  • Does parameter sharing help? a multitask recommender experiment (2025-05-10): I built the same recommender four times to find out whether sharing parameters across tasks helps. It turned out the loss weighting mattered more than the sharing, which is a known and slightly annoying axis.
  • Starting a blog (2025-05-01): Why this page exists, and what to expect.
